## Abstract

Rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor with robust preclinical lifespan extension data, is a leading candidate for pharmacological geroprotection, yet its translation to human healthspan remains contested.

This synthesis evaluates the current human evidence base by integrating findings from randomized controlled trials and observational cohorts that assessed rapamycin's effects on cardiometabolic and immune endpoints relevant to aging.

Across the curated corpus, null or mixed findings dominate the cardiometabolic outcome class, creating significant tension with the drug's mechanistic promise.

The PEARL trial, enrolling approximately 80 adults (Moel 2025), reported no significant effect of rapamycin on body mass index over one year, with placebo groups showing no change.

In contrast, the RAPA-EX-01 trial in older adults found mixed results, with placebo groups showing both significant decreases and increases in HbA1c (p < 0.001, p = 0.025, p = 0.012), indicating complex interactions between rapamycin, exercise, and glycemic control.

These clinical findings contrast with observational data suggesting rapamycin may enhance immune resilience against DNA damage and decrease mTOR signaling within five weeks, pointing to a potential mechanistic pathway distinct from direct cardiometabolic modulation.

The disagreement between the null cardiometabolic findings in the PEARL trial and the mixed results in RAPA-EX-01 constitutes a primary tension in the evidence base, with a severity rating of 4.

Furthermore, the unclear effect direction from the observational cohort (Kell 2026) adds a third layer of discordance, highlighting the challenge of interpreting indirect evidence against direct trial outcomes.

The case for rapamycin as a geroprotector is thus incomplete: while preclinical data show lifespan extension of approximately 14% in male mice (Harrison 2009), human RCT evidence for cardiometabolic healthspan benefits remains sparse and inconsistent.

The boundary conditions—such as optimal dosing, duration, and target population—remain to be established, and future trials must reconcile the mechanistic plausibility of mTOR inhibition with the observed functional tradeoffs in human aging.

## Introduction

Population aging represents one of the defining demographic transformations of the twenty-first century, bringing with it a rising burden of chronic disease, functional decline, and healthcare expenditure that strains both economies and individual lives. As life expectancy has increased across high-income nations, the years gained have not always been accompanied by sustained health, leaving a growing gap between lifespan and healthspan that carries profound human and economic consequences. The accumulation of age-related conditions — including sarcopenia, cardiometabolic dysfunction, cognitive decline, and immune senescence — does not occur in isolation; rather, these phenotypes share overlapping biological substrates rooted in the fundamental biology of aging itself. This observation has prompted a search for interventions that might target upstream aging mechanisms rather than individual downstream diseases, with the hope of compressing morbidity and extending the period of life spent in good functional health. Pharmacological geroprotectors have emerged as a particularly compelling class of candidate interventions, given their potential scalability and the possibility of complementing lifestyle-based approaches that, while effective, face well-documented adherence challenges in older populations. Among the molecules that have attracted the most sustained scientific attention in this space, rapamycin — an mTOR inhibitor with a decades-long clinical history in transplant medicine — has become a focal point for both preclinical excitement and clinical scrutiny. The question of whether rapamycin can meaningfully improve healthspan metrics in humans remains one of the most actively debated issues in translational geroscience, and the stakes of answering it correctly are considerable.

Rapamycin, also known as sirolimus, belongs to the class of mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors and exerts its primary pharmacological effect through inhibition of the mTORC1 complex, a central integrator of nutrient availability, growth factor signaling, and cellular metabolic state. The mTOR pathway has been implicated in aging biology across multiple model organisms, and its inhibition has been proposed to activate autophagy, reduce cellular senescence, modulate inflammatory signaling, and enhance stress resilience — mechanisms that collectively align with the hallmarks of aging framework. In preclinical studies, rapamycin has demonstrated lifespan extension in heterogeneous mouse populations, with Harrison 2009 reporting median lifespan increases of approximately 14% in males and 9% in females through the NIA Interventions Testing Program, findings that galvanized interest in translating this molecule to human aging contexts. The clinical history of rapamycin spans decades in solid-organ transplant immunosuppression, where whole-blood trough concentrations are typically maintained in the range described by Kahan 2000, but the dosing strategies being explored for geroprotective purposes differ markedly from transplant protocols. Early human aging trials adopted weekly low-dose approaches, with Mannick 2014 employing a regimen involving 5 mg administered intermittently, seeking to capture mTORC1-selective inhibition while minimizing off-target mTORC2 disruption. From an accessibility standpoint, rapamycin is available as a generic medication with a well-characterized safety profile, which lowers barriers to clinical investigation compared to novel compounds, though the absence of patent protection for aging indications may paradoxically reduce commercial incentive for large-scale development. The drug thus occupies a unique position in the geroprotective pharmacopeia: mechanistically compelling, clinically familiar, yet still lacking the definitive human evidence needed to establish its role in healthspan extension.

The human randomized controlled trial evidence for rapamycin in aging-related outcomes remains limited in scope but is beginning to accumulate, spanning cardiometabolic, immune, and functional domains with notable heterogeneity in design, population, and endpoints. The PEARL trial, reported by Moel 2025, enrolled approximately 80 adults and assessed the influence of rapamycin on safety and healthspan metrics over one year, with the study finding that body mass index did not change meaningfully in the placebo group and that the overall cardiometabolic signal was null — a result that tempers enthusiasm generated by preclinical data. In contrast, the RAPA-EX-01 trial reported by Stanfield 2026 examined the combination of exercise and weekly sirolimus in older adults and produced a mixed pattern of findings: HbA1c decreased significantly in some comparisons (p < 0.001, p = 0.025) but also increased in others (p = 0.012), suggesting that the interaction between rapamycin and exercise physiology may be more complex than either intervention alone. An observational cohort analysis by Kell 2026 provided indirect evidence that rapamycin may exert geroprotective effects in the aging human immune system by enhancing resilience against DNA damage, with evidence of decreased mTOR signaling observed after five weeks, though the observational design limits causal inference. Taken together, these three studies illustrate the current state of the field: a small number of trials with divergent endpoints, varying durations, and populations that differ in age, health status, and concomitant interventions, making it difficult to synthesize a coherent picture of rapamycin's clinical efficacy. The endpoint diversity is itself a challenge — cardiometabolic markers, immune function, body composition, and safety assessments each capture different facets of healthspan, and no single trial has yet integrated these domains into a unified assessment framework. The evidence base, while growing, remains insufficient to support definitive clinical recommendations, and the tensions between null, mixed, and mechanistically suggestive findings demand careful interpretation rather than premature closure.

Several unresolved questions continue to shape the trajectory of rapamycin research in human aging, and these questions extend well beyond the simple binary of whether the drug 'works' or does not. The tension between mechanistic plausibility and functional translation is perhaps the most fundamental: while mTOR inhibition has clear biological rationale supported by decades of preclinical work, the translation of pathway modulation into clinically meaningful healthspan improvements in humans has proven far less straightforward than the preclinical narrative might suggest. A particularly concerning finding from the RAPA-EX-01 trial is the possibility that rapamycin may blunt beneficial exercise adaptations, as the mixed HbA1c results reported by Stanfield 2026 raise the specter of a drug-lifestyle interaction that could undermine one of the most potent non-pharmacological geroprotective strategies available. Population specificity represents another critical gap — it remains uncertain whether certain subgroups of older adults, defined by baseline frailty status, metabolic health, immune function, or genetic background, might derive greater benefit from rapamycin than others, or whether the drug's risk-benefit profile differs across the spectrum of aging phenotypes. The question of optimal dosing and treatment duration also remains open: intermittent weekly dosing appears to mitigate some safety concerns, but the relationship between dose, exposure duration, and healthspan outcomes has not been systematically characterized in human trials. Furthermore, the boundary conditions of rapamycin's effects — under what circumstances the drug produces benefit, under what circumstances it produces harm, and under what circumstances it produces no detectable effect — are poorly defined and may vary across outcome domains. Until these boundary conditions are mapped with greater precision, the clinical utility of rapamycin as a geroprotector will remain uncertain, and the gap between its mechanistic promise and its demonstrated human efficacy will continue to generate legitimate scientific debate.

## Background

The geroscience hypothesis posits that the biological mechanisms underlying aging are themselves the primary drivers of the chronic diseases that dominate morbidity and mortality in later life. Rather than targeting individual diseases one at a time, this framework proposes that modulating the fundamental biology of aging could simultaneously delay or prevent multiple age-related pathologies. This intellectual lineage draws on decades of comparative biology showing that lifespan is a plastic trait, modifiable by genetic, dietary, and pharmacological interventions across species. The formal articulation of the hallmarks of aging — including genomic instability, telomere attrition, altered intercellular communication, and cellular senescence — provided a conceptual scaffold for identifying tractable molecular targets. Among these, the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway emerged as a central node, integrating nutrient sensing, autophagy, protein synthesis, and immune regulation. The convergence of epidemiological, preclinical, and mechanistic evidence around mTOR inhibition catalyzed a wave of translational interest that now shapes the clinical-trial landscape for aging interventions. Regulatory agencies have begun to grapple with the implications: if aging is a modifiable process, then endpoints beyond traditional disease-specific measures become relevant, and trial designs must accommodate long time horizons and heterogeneous populations. This background sets the stage for evaluating rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor with a complex pharmacological profile, as a candidate geroprotective agent in human populations.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Kell 2026`_

The human evidence base for rapamycin as a geroprotective intervention remains nascent and fragmented. Rapamycin (sirolimus) has an established clinical history in solid-organ transplantation, where it is used as an immunosuppressant at trough concentrations typically targeted to the 5-15 ng/mL range (Kahan 2000), but these doses and continuous-dosing schedules are far higher and more sustained than those proposed for aging indications. Early-stage human studies have explored whether low-dose, intermittent rapamycin — often at weekly dosing of 5 mg or less (Mannick 2014) — can modulate immune aging biomarkers without the adverse metabolic effects seen at immunosuppressive doses. An observational cohort study reported that rapamycin may enhance resilience against DNA damage in the aging human immune system, with evidence of decreased mTOR signaling after approximately 5 weeks of exposure (Kell 2026), suggesting that even short-duration exposure can engage relevant biological pathways. However, the translation from immune biomarker modulation to clinically meaningful healthspan outcomes remains poorly characterized. The PEARL trial, enrolling approximately 80 adults (Moel 2025), represents one of the first dedicated geroscience RCTs of rapamycin, yet its published findings on cardiometabolic endpoints showed no significant change in body mass index in the placebo group, and the overall effect profile was null. These early human data raise critical questions about whether the robust preclinical signal for lifespan extension will translate into detectable healthspan gains in humans, or whether the effect sizes, treatment durations, and population characteristics required for benefit differ fundamentally from animal models.

The clinical-trial landscape for rapamycin in aging-relevant populations is small but growing, and the available evidence reveals a pattern of mixed or null findings that complicates the translational narrative. The PEARL trial (Moel 2025) was a randomized, placebo-controlled study enrolling approximately 80 adults that assessed rapamycin's influence on safety and healthspan metrics over one year; its cardiometabolic outcomes were largely null, with no significant change in body mass index observed in the placebo arm. The RAPA-EX-01 trial (Stanfield 2026) took a different design approach, combining exercise with weekly sirolimus in older adults in a double-blind, placebo-controlled framework; this study reported mixed findings across its cardiometabolic endpoints, with statistically significant changes in HbA1c in multiple comparisons (p < 0.001, p = 0.025, p = 0.012, p = 0.036, p = 0.030), though the direction and interpretation of these effects remain contested. The inclusion of exercise as a co-intervention in RAPA-EX-01 introduces a critical confound: it becomes difficult to disentangle the independent contribution of rapamycin from the well-established cardiometabolic benefits of physical activity. An observational cohort study (Kell 2026) provided indirect evidence that rapamycin modulates immune-system mTOR signaling in aging humans, but the absence of clinical or functional endpoints limits its relevance to healthspan claims. The tension between these studies — null cardiometabolic findings in PEARL, mixed findings in RAPA-EX-01, and mechanistic but clinically indirect observations in the cohort study — constitutes the core evidence gap that this synthesis addresses. Collectively, these trials enroll modest sample sizes, employ heterogeneous dosing regimens, and follow participants for durations that may be insufficient to detect healthspan-relevant outcomes, underscoring the preliminary nature of the current evidence base.

Several open methodological questions constrain the interpretation of rapamycin's geroprotective potential and the design of future trials. Endpoint selection remains a central challenge: functional outcomes such as gait speed, grip strength, and frailty indices carry direct clinical relevance but require large samples and long follow-up to detect effects, whereas surrogate biomarkers such as HbA1c, inflammatory cytokines, and mTOR-pathway activity are more responsive but may not predict meaningful healthspan gains. The choice of endpoints is further complicated by the fact that clinically important thresholds — such as gait speed below 0.8 m/s (Studenski 2011) or grip strength below 27 kg in men and 16 kg in women (Cruz-Jentoft 2019) — define risk strata that may respond differently to mTOR inhibition depending on baseline functional status. Population heterogeneity adds another layer of complexity: diabetic and non-diabetic participants may exhibit divergent cardiometabolic responses to rapamycin, given the drug's known effects on glucose homeostasis, and baseline frailty status may modify both the risk-benefit ratio and the likelihood of detecting treatment effects. The mechanism-to-clinic gap remains substantial — rapamycin's ability to decrease mTOR signaling over short durations (Kell 2026) does not guarantee that sustained or intermittent dosing will produce durable functional improvements. Treatment duration is itself an unresolved variable: the 2-week threshold for mTORC2 disruption motivates intermittent dosing, but the optimal frequency, duration, and cumulative exposure for healthspan benefit in humans are unknown. Interactions with concurrent interventions, particularly exercise, represent both a confound and an opportunity; the RAPA-EX-01 trial's combination of sirolimus with structured exercise (Stanfield 2026) suggests that synergistic or antagonistic effects may emerge, but the evidence is insufficient to guide clinical practice. Finally, the attrition rates typical of long-duration RCTs in older adults — estimated at approximately 20% (Schulz 2010) — pose a statistical-power challenge that is amplified by the modest sample sizes of current rapamycin trials, raising the possibility that true effects may exist but remain undetectable with available study designs.

## Cross-Domain Synthesis

The most prominent tension in the rapamycin anti-aging corpus is the discordance between the null cardiometabolic findings from the PEARL trial and the mixed but statistically significant signals from the RAPA-EX-01 trial. Moel 2025 reports that after one year of rapamycin administration in adults, there was no meaningful change in body mass index in the treatment group compared to placebo, with the placebo arm showing no change in body mass index across multiple reported subgroups. In direct contrast, Stanfield 2026 reports multiple statistically significant changes in HbA1c in older adults receiving weekly sirolimus alongside an exercise intervention, with p-values ranging from p < 0.001 to p = 0.036 across different comparisons. The mechanistic explanation for this disagreement likely lies in the interaction between rapamycin's metabolic effects and the physiological context of the treated population. Rapamycin's inhibition of mTORC1 is known to alter glucose metabolism and insulin signaling, and these effects may be more pronounced or clinically detectable in older adults with pre-existing metabolic dysregulation or in those receiving concurrent exercise stimulus that amplifies metabolic flux. The boundary condition may therefore be that rapamycin's cardiometabolic effects are only detectable when the population has sufficient metabolic reserve to respond or when a co-intervention like exercise creates the physiological demand that unmasks mTOR-related metabolic shifts. The PEARL trial's broader adult population, including those with a body mass index distribution where placebo showed no change in body mass index at 35.1%, may have been too heterogeneous or metabolically stable to reveal effects that emerge under more specific conditions. Resolving this tension would require a head-to-head trial design that systematically varies both the metabolic baseline of participants and the presence or absence of exercise co-intervention, isolating whether rapamycin's cardiometabolic signal is population-dependent, context-dependent, or both.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Stanfield 2026`_

A second critical tension exists between the mechanistic evidence for rapamycin's geroprotective action on the immune system and the absence of corresponding functional clinical endpoints in the available RCTs. Kell 2026 provides observational cohort evidence that rapamycin enhances resilience against DNA damage in the ageing human immune system, suggesting a plausible biological mechanism by which mTOR inhibition could slow immune senescence and, by extension, reduce age-related disease burden. This mechanistic signal is consistent with the broader preclinical literature showing rapamycin-mediated lifespan extension in model organisms, where Harrison 2009 demonstrated median lifespan extension in heterogeneous mice. However, neither Moel 2025 nor Stanfield 2026 reports clinical endpoints that would translate this immune resilience into tangible healthspan outcomes such as reduced infection rates, improved vaccine response, or lower incidence of age-related inflammatory conditions. The tension here is between biological plausibility and clinical demonstration: the immune system finding provides a mechanistic rationale, but the RCTs were not designed or powered to capture immune-specific functional endpoints. The boundary condition is that mechanistic biomarker improvements, even when statistically meaningful, do not automatically confer clinical benefit unless the downstream pathway connects to a patient-relevant outcome. Kell 2026's finding that rapamycin decreases mTOR signaling at 5 weeks provides a pharmacodynamic proof of concept, but the durability and clinical significance of this immune modulation over longer time horizons remains untested in controlled trials. Evidence that would resolve this tension includes an RCT specifically designed to measure immune function endpoints such as vaccine antibody titers, infection incidence, or inflammatory biomarker trajectories alongside the cardiometabolic measures already reported.

  _Cited: `Kell 2026`, `Moel 2025`_

A third tension emerges when considering the dose and duration of rapamycin exposure across the different study designs and their reported outcomes. This pharmacological nuance has driven the field toward intermittent, low-dose regimens, with early human aging trials using weekly dosing at approximately 5 mg (Mannick 2014). Stanfield 2026 employed weekly sirolimus in combination with exercise and reported mixed HbA1c results, with some comparisons showing placebo-related decreases in HbA1c at p < 0.001 and p = 0.025, while others showed placebo-related increases at p = 0.012. This pattern of mixed directional effects is consistent with the hypothesis that intermittent dosing may partially avoid mTORC2 disruption but does not fully eliminate metabolic perturbation, particularly in the context of exercise where glucose dynamics are already in flux. Moel 2025's PEARL trial, by contrast, reported null cardiometabolic findings over one year, which could indicate either that the dosing regimen successfully avoided metabolic harm or that the trial lacked the sensitivity to detect subtle metabolic shifts. The tension is whether the mixed Stanfield 2026 signals represent a genuine but modest metabolic cost of rapamycin that the PEARL trial was underpowered to detect, or whether the exercise co-intervention in RAPA-EX-01 created a metabolic context that amplified rapamycin's effects in ways that would not occur in sedentary populations. The boundary condition likely relates to the specific dosing schedule and whether concurrent physical activity modulates the metabolic response to mTOR inhibition. Evidence that would resolve this includes pharmacokinetic data from both trials showing actual trough concentrations relative to the transplant-era reference range of 5-15 ng/mL (Kahan 2000), which would clarify whether the two trials achieved comparable drug exposure despite their different designs.

The overarching synthesis tension concerns the inferential gap between preclinical longevity evidence and the current human RCT landscape for rapamycin as a geroprotective agent. The preclinical case is strong: Harrison 2009 demonstrated that rapamycin extends median lifespan in heterogeneous mice by approximately 14% in males and 9% in females, providing the foundational evidence that mTOR inhibition can delay aging at the organismal level. This animal-model evidence has generated substantial enthusiasm for rapamycin as a potential human longevity intervention, and the mechanistic findings from Kell 2026 regarding immune system DNA damage resilience provide a plausible translational bridge. However, the human RCT evidence as currently constituted does not support a longevity or broad healthspan claim. Moel 2025's PEARL trial, with approximately 80 participants enrolled across treatment and placebo arms, reported null cardiometabolic findings after one year, and Stanfield 2026's mixed HbA1c results in older adults do not constitute evidence of healthspan extension. The tension is that the preclinical-to-clinical translation gap remains wide: model-organism lifespan extension does not predict human clinical benefit, and the available human trials were neither designed nor powered to detect mortality or major healthspan endpoints. The boundary condition is that rapamycin's geroprotective potential in humans remains hypothetical until trials are conducted with sufficient duration, sample size, and composite healthspan endpoints to detect the kinds of benefits observed in mice. The PEARL trial's enrollment of approximately 80 adults (Moel 2025) is orders of magnitude below what would be required for a mortality endpoint, and even the cardiometabolic signals may require longer follow-up to manifest. Evidence that would resolve this tension includes large-scale, multi-year RCTs with composite endpoints encompassing physical function, cognitive performance, immune function, and cardiometabolic health, ideally with pre-specified subgroup analyses by metabolic baseline and exercise status.

## Discussion

The most robust signal that emerges from this curated synthesis is the consistent failure of rapamycin to produce clinically meaningful cardiometabolic benefit in human randomized controlled trials. The RAPA-EX-01 trial, which combined weekly sirolimus with structured exercise in older adults, reported a mixed pattern: while some cardiometabolic markers shifted, the directionality was inconsistent, with the placebo group showing both decreases and increases in HbA1c across different statistical comparisons (Stanfield 2026). When these two direct-evidence sources are placed alongside the observational cohort work by Kell 2026, which reported unclear effects on cardiometabolic outcomes despite documenting decreased mTOR signaling at five weeks, the overall picture is one of mechanistic engagement without downstream clinical translation. We interpret this convergence as the strongest finding in the current evidence base: rapamycin's capacity to modulate mTOR signaling in human tissues does not, in the trials conducted to date, translate into the cardiometabolic improvements that preclinical models would predict. This is not a null finding in the trivial sense of underpowered trials; rather, it is a pattern of null and mixed results across multiple independent research groups using different dosing regimens and population profiles. The consistency of this pattern across the three reviewed papers warrants serious attention from the field, because it challenges the assumption that mTOR inhibition is a viable standalone pathway for cardiometabolic healthspan extension in humans.

Where the evidence becomes genuinely mixed is in the tension between the null cardiometabolic findings and the mechanistic plausibility that rapamycin engages geroprotective pathways at the molecular level. Kell 2026 provides observational evidence that rapamycin enhances resilience against DNA damage in the ageing human immune system, with decreased mTOR signaling documented at five weeks, yet this mechanistic signal did not translate into clear cardiometabolic benefit in the same study's clinical endpoints. Stanfield 2026 reported statistically significant changes in HbA1c (with p-values ranging from p < 0.001 to p = 0.036 across different comparisons), but the directionality was mixed: the placebo group showed both decreases and increases in HbA1c, making it difficult to attribute a net clinical benefit to the rapamycin-plus-exercise intervention. This tension, which we rate at severity 4 in the disagreement matrix, suggests that mTOR modulation may be necessary but not sufficient for cardiometabolic improvement, or alternatively that the dosing regimens tested have not yet optimized the therapeutic window. One reading of this pattern is that rapamycin's geroprotective effects may operate through pathways that are not captured by conventional cardiometabolic endpoints such as BMI and HbA1c. The evidence appears consistent with a model in which rapamycin engages cellular stress-response and DNA-repair mechanisms, but these molecular benefits may not manifest as measurable improvements in the clinical markers that trials have prioritized. This interpretation remains qualified by the small sample sizes and relatively short follow-up periods characteristic of the current trial literature. Until larger trials with longer follow-up and more granular mechanistic endpoints are conducted, the mechanistic-to-clinical translation gap will remain the central unresolved question in rapamycin geroscience.

The gap between mechanistic plausibility and clinical-endpoint evidence is perhaps the most consequential tension for the field, and it deserves careful unpacking. Preclinical work, exemplified by the Harrison 2009 Interventions Testing Program finding of approximately 14% median lifespan extension in male heterogeneous mice and 9% in females, established rapamycin as the most robust pharmacological geroprotector in animal models. This preclinical signal motivated the human trials reviewed here, yet the translation has been disappointing. The PEARL trial (Moel 2025) and RAPA-EX-01 (Stanfield 2026) both tested rapamycin against direct clinical endpoints—body mass index and HbA1c respectively—and found null or mixed results. Meanwhile, Kell 2026 documented mechanistic engagement (decreased mTOR signaling, enhanced DNA-damage resilience) in an observational design without clear clinical-endpoint confirmation. We interpret this divergence as evidence that the field may have prematurely assumed that mTOR inhibition, which is a proximal molecular event, would cascade into distal clinical benefits through a straightforward causal chain. The reality appears more complex: mTOR signaling is embedded in a network of compensatory pathways, and chronic inhibition beyond approximately two weeks may trigger mTORC2 disruption that offsets mTORC1-mediated benefits. This mechanistic complexity may explain why intermittent low-dose regimens, such as the weekly dosing approach used in early human aging trials (Mannick 2014), have not yet produced the clinical signals that the preclinical literature would predict. The field therefore faces a design challenge: trials must be long enough and large enough to detect benefits that may emerge through indirect or delayed pathways, while also incorporating mechanistic endpoints that can distinguish genuine geroprotection from pharmacological noise.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Stanfield 2026`, `Kell 2026`_

Population specificity introduces another layer of complexity that the current evidence base only partially addresses. The PEARL trial enrolled approximately 80 adults (Moel 2025), a sample size that, while reasonable for an initial safety-and-feasibility study, may be insufficient to detect subgroup effects in populations with varying baseline cardiometabolic risk. The RAPA-EX-01 trial focused specifically on older adults (Stanfield 2026), a population in whom the competing risks of polypharmacy, multimorbidity, and age-related physiological decline may attenuate or mask rapamycin's effects. Kell 2026 studied adults more broadly without stratification by age or comorbidity burden, leaving open the question of whether rapamycin's geroprotective effects are concentrated in specific subpopulations. One reading of the available evidence is that rapamycin may be most beneficial in individuals with elevated baseline mTOR activity—such as those with insulin resistance or chronic inflammation—rather than in the general ageing population. This hypothesis is consistent with the broader geroscience principle that interventions targeting fundamental ageing mechanisms should show the greatest absolute benefit in those with the highest biological age or disease burden. However, the trials reviewed here did not stratify by baseline mTOR activity, inflammatory markers, or metabolic phenotype, limiting our ability to test this hypothesis directly. Future trials should incorporate pre-specified subgroup analyses by baseline risk profile, and should consider enrolling enriched populations where the signal-to-noise ratio for geroprotective effects may be more favorable. Until such stratified evidence is available, the question of who benefits most from rapamycin remains uncertain and context-dependent.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Stanfield 2026`, `Kell 2026`_

Methodological considerations across the three reviewed studies illuminate several design features that may have limited the detection of rapamycin's clinical effects. Sample size is a primary concern: the PEARL trial enrolled approximately 80 participants (Moel 2025), and while this is adequate for safety assessment, it provides limited statistical power for detecting modest effect sizes on clinical endpoints. Attrition in long-duration trials of older adults can reach approximately 20% (Schulz 2010), further eroding effective sample size and statistical power. The RAPA-EX-01 trial (Stanfield 2026) combined rapamycin with exercise, introducing a multifactorial design that complicates attribution of observed effects to the pharmacological intervention versus the behavioral component. Endpoint selection also warrants critical reflection: body mass index and HbA1c are standard cardiometabolic markers, but they may not be the most sensitive indicators of geroprotective benefit. Follow-up duration is another limitation: the five-week mTOR-signaling assessment in Kell 2026 captures early mechanistic engagement but cannot speak to sustained clinical benefit over months or years. We suggest that future trials should aim for follow-up periods of at least one to two years, incorporate composite endpoints that span cardiometabolic, immune, and functional domains, and include mechanistic biomarkers (such as DNA-damage markers, senescence-associated secretory phenotype factors, and immune-cell phenotyping) alongside conventional clinical endpoints. These design improvements would help bridge the gap between the promising preclinical signal and the mixed human evidence reviewed here.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Stanfield 2026`, `Kell 2026`_

The clinical implications of this synthesis are necessarily preliminary, but they point toward several actionable priorities. First, the consistent null and mixed cardiometabolic findings across the PEARL and RAPA-EX-01 trials suggest that rapamycin should not be prescribed off-label for cardiometabolic healthspan extension on the basis of current evidence. Second, the mechanistic signals documented by Kell 2026—enhanced DNA-damage resilience and decreased mTOR signaling—warrant further investigation in trials designed specifically to test immune and cellular-ageing endpoints rather than conventional cardiometabolic markers. Third, the field should move toward dose-optimization studies that systematically compare intermittent versus continuous dosing, given the evidence that chronic rapamycin exposure beyond approximately two weeks may disrupt mTORC2 and offset mTORC1-mediated benefits. Fourth, the use of transplant-immunosuppression dosing targets—such as sirolimus trough concentrations of 5–15 ng/mL (Kahan 2000)—as a reference for aging-relevant dosing may be inappropriate, since geroprotective regimens likely require far lower and less frequent exposure. Fifth, the integration of rapamycin with lifestyle interventions, as tested in RAPA-EX-01 (Stanfield 2026), merits further exploration, since the interaction between pharmacological mTOR inhibition and exercise-induced mTOR activation is biologically complex and may produce synergistic or antagonistic effects depending on timing and dose. In our view, the rapamycin geroscience narrative is at an inflection point: the preclinical case remains compelling, with lifespan extensions of approximately 14% in male mice (Harrison 2009), but the human evidence is insufficient to support clinical adoption. The next generation of trials must be larger, longer, and more mechanistically informed if the field is to move from biological plausibility to clinical proof.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Stanfield 2026`, `Kell 2026`_

## Limitations

The curated corpus is limited to three reference papers, all of which focus on cardiometabolic outcomes, and none of which report long-term mortality or hard clinical-event endpoints. The largest trial, PEARL, enrolled approximately 80 adults (Moel 2025), and the RAPA-EX-01 trial studied older adults on a weekly sirolimus regimen alongside exercise, yet neither was powered for survival or age-related disease incidence. Consequently, the headline conclusion that rapamycin's anti-aging case remains incomplete rests on a narrow evidence base that excludes the very outcomes—mortality, cardiovascular events, cancer incidence—that would be most decisive for a geroprotective claim. The tension between Moel 2025's null cardiometabolic findings and Stanfield 2026's mixed HbA1c results cannot be resolved without larger, longer-duration trials that track hard endpoints. This corpus-level gap means the synthesis can speak to mechanistic plausibility and short-term safety but not to the clinical efficacy that would justify widespread off-label use.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Stanfield 2026`_

Several outcome domains that are central to the rapamycin-for-aging narrative are either absent or represented by only a single receipt, creating single-trial generalization risk. Immune resilience, for instance, is addressed solely by Kell 2026's observational cohort showing decreased mTOR signaling after five weeks, with no replication within the corpus. Functional endpoints relevant to aging—such as gait speed, grip strength, and fall incidence—are entirely unmeasured, even though canonical thresholds exist for clinical significance (Studenski 2011; Cruz-Jentoft 2019). The absence of any receipt reporting gait speed below 0.8 m/s (Studenski 2011) or grip strength below 27 kg in men (Cruz-Jentoft 2019) means the synthesis cannot assess whether rapamycin preserves mobility or muscle function. This means that for every outcome class beyond cardiometabolic markers, the evidence base consists of at most one study, precluding internal replication.

  _Cited: `Kell 2026`_

Population specificity further constrains external validity. The PEARL trial enrolled approximately 80 adults (Moel 2025) and RAPA-EX-01 enrolled older adults, but neither trial's enrollment criteria are described in sufficient detail to determine whether participants had diabetes, obesity, or other comorbidities that would modulate rapamycin's risk–benefit profile. No receipt in the corpus reports outcomes stratified by baseline HbA1c relative to the ADA 2024 target of 7%, nor by body-mass index relative to the WHO 2000 overweight threshold of 25 kg/m² or obesity threshold of 30 kg/m². The observational Kell 2026 cohort adds mechanistic data but does not specify whether participants were immunocompromised, transplant recipients, or healthy volunteers, limiting its interpretive value. As a result, the synthesis cannot determine whether rapamycin's effects are consistent across metabolic phenotypes or confined to specific subpopulations. This is a critical gap because the preclinical Harrison 2009 lifespan data—showing approximately 14% median lifespan extension in male heterogeneous mice—were generated in genetically diverse animals, and human translation may depend on analogous population heterogeneity.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Kell 2026`_

The corpus also lacks dose-ranging and regimen-comparison studies, leaving a mechanistic-to-clinical gap unresolved. Preclinical work established that chronic rapamycin dosing beyond approximately 2 weeks disrupts mTORC2 in addition to mTORC1 (Lamming 2012), providing a mechanistic rationale for the intermittent weekly protocols used in human aging trials, yet no receipt in the corpus directly compares intermittent versus chronic dosing in humans. The early human aging literature used weekly low-dose regimens of approximately 5 mg (Mannick 2014), and transplant medicine targets trough concentrations of 5–15 ng/mL (Kahan 2000), but the curated trials do not report pharmacokinetic data or trough levels, making it impossible to assess whether the doses used were in a geroprotectively relevant range. Furthermore, the preclinical Harrison 2009 finding of approximately 9% median lifespan extension in female mice—companion to the 14% male effect—raises the question of sex-dependent responses, yet none of the human receipts report sex-stratified outcomes. Until dose–response and sex-stratified human data are available, the translation from the robust preclinical lifespan evidence to clinical geroprotection remains speculative.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Stanfield 2026`, `Kell 2026`_

## Conclusion

This synthesis of three curated reference papers suggests that rapamycin's potential as a geroprotective agent in humans presents a complex and incomplete picture, where mechanistic plausibility coexists with mixed or sparse clinical evidence. The strongest supporting signal emerges from the RAPA-EX-01 trial, where Stanfield 2026 reported statistically significant effects on HbA1c in older adults, with p-values including p < 0.001, p = 0.025, and p = 0.012, indicating that the drug may exert context-dependent cardiometabolic influences when combined with exercise. This is complemented by Kell 2026, whose observational data suggests rapamycin may enhance resilience against DNA damage in the aging human immune system, offering a plausible mechanistic bridge to preclinical lifespan extension data, such as the Harrison 2009 finding of approximately 14% median lifespan extension in male mice. However, the most direct human RCT evidence, the PEARL trial (Moel 2025), presents a significant counterpoint: in a cohort of approximately 80 adults, the study found null effects on body mass index, with the placebo group showing no change in 35.1% of participants. This null result, when juxtaposed with the mixed findings from Stanfield 2026, creates a core tension (severity 4) that undermines a simple, unified narrative of rapamycin's efficacy. The boundary conditions for any potential benefit—such as optimal dosing regimen, duration, and patient selection—remain entirely undefined. The field's pivot to intermittent, low-dose protocols, informed by findings like Lamming 2012's observation that chronic dosing beyond approximately 2 weeks disrupts mTORC2, represents a critical methodological refinement, but human trials have yet to systematically map this parameter space.

  _Cited: `Stanfield 2026`, `Kell 2026`, `Moel 2025`_

Therefore, the evidence supports a hypothesis that rapamycin may modulate specific healthspan-related pathways in humans, but the current clinical trial landscape is insufficient to confirm a net geroprotective benefit. The recommended next step is the execution of larger, longer-duration RCTs designed to resolve the identified tensions, particularly the disagreement between null and mixed cardiometabolic outcomes. Future trials must incorporate precise, intermittent dosing schedules, such as the weekly low-dose regimen used by Mannick 2014, and measure a broader panel of functional and resilience endpoints beyond traditional metabolic markers. The synthesis underscores that the case for rapamycin as an anti-aging intervention in humans is not yet constituted; it remains to be confirmed in adequately powered trials that can establish efficacy, define the therapeutic window, and characterize the risk-benefit profile for a non-transplant population. Until such data emerge, any clinical application for longevity purposes appears premature.

  _Cited: `Moel 2025`, `Stanfield 2026`_
