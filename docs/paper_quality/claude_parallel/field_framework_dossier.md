## 1. Mannick — "low-dose intermittent rapalogs improve immune aging in humans"

**Core thesis.** Joan Mannick (Tornado Therapeutics; previously resTORbio; Novartis aging unit) argues that the human translation of rapamycin/rapalog geroprotection is dose-regimen-dependent and immune-aging-first. Her positions, made concrete across PIE/RTB101 trials, are: (a) weekly low-dose rapalog dosing engages mTORC1 without sustained mTORC2 disruption; (b) the most translatable proof-of-concept endpoint in humans is improved response to influenza vaccination and reduced incidence of respiratory tract infections in older adults; (c) rapamycin/rapalog skeptics conflate immunosuppressive transplant dosing with intermittent geroprotective dosing.

**Anchor papers.**
- Mannick et al., *Sci Transl Med* 2014, "mTOR inhibition improves immune function in the elderly" (everolimus; influenza vaccine response; DOI: 10.1126/scitranslmed.3009892, PMID: 25540326 — in current bundle).
- Mannick et al., *Sci Transl Med* 2018, "TORC1 inhibition enhances immune function and reduces infections in the elderly" (RTB101 + everolimus PIE-1) — verify before quote.
- Mannick et al., *Lancet Healthy Longevity* 2021, RTB101 phase 3 readout — verify before quote.
- Mannick & Lamming, *Nature Aging* 2023 — joint review centering mTORC1-benefit / mTORC2-toxicity boundary (referenced in the gap-analysis doc).

**What Mannick would criticize in our paper.**
1. "Cardiometabolic-only outcome class" framing erases the actual translatable endpoint (immune aging / infection rate) on which the field has positive randomized signal.
2. Treating Moel 2025 (PEARL, BMI as primary) as the load-bearing human RCT understates the *negative result on the wrong endpoint* problem: rapamycin was never expected to reduce BMI; null on BMI is not null on geroprotection.
3. Citing Mannick 2014 only as a dose anchor ("approximately 5 mg") flattens its content into pharmacology while ignoring its primary-outcome evidence on vaccine response.

**What our corpus supports / challenges.**
- *Supports Mannick:* Stanfield 2026 (RAPA-EX-01) operates within Mannick's intermittent low-dose paradigm; Kell 2026 documents short-duration mTOR engagement consistent with her mechanism→biomarker prediction.
- *Challenges Mannick:* Stanfield 2026's mixed HbA1c signal (placebo-arm directionality) is awkward for a clean immune-aging-first narrative; if rapamycin perturbs glycemic control even at intermittent dose, "purely mTORC1-selective" oversimplifies.
- *Post-rescue check:* with 34 receipts now admitted, expect Mannick 2014 + Mannick 2018 to enter as direct receipts rather than background tokens; the immune-aging outcome class becomes addressable rather than absent.

**Proposed engagement section language.**

> Mannick's framework, formalized across the PIE/RTB101 program (Mannick 2014; Mannick 2018; Mannick & Lamming 2023), holds that the translatable proof-of-concept for rapalog geroprotection is improved immune function in older adults under weekly intermittent dosing — not cardiometabolic surrogates. Our corpus is consistent with her framework on dose regimen (weekly sirolimus, Stanfield 2026; five-week mTOR engagement, Kell 2026) but does not yet contain a directly-comparable infection-rate or vaccine-response endpoint, leaving the load-bearing Mannick prediction untested in the synthesised receipts. We therefore neither support nor refute Mannick's central translational claim; we observe that the cardiometabolic-only outcome class of the current corpus understates the field's strongest positive human signal.

## 2. Lamming — "mTORC2 disruption is the off-target tax on chronic rapamycin"

**Core thesis.** Dudley Lamming (UW–Madison) argues that the geroprotective benefit of rapamycin is mTORC1-mediated but the *toxicity* — particularly hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and male reproductive effects — is mTORC2-mediated and emerges with chronic continuous dosing beyond approximately two weeks. The framework predicts a therapeutic window: intermittent dosing or dose holidays preserve mTORC1 inhibition while sparing mTORC2.

**Anchor papers.**
- Lamming et al., *Science* 2012, "Rapamycin-induced insulin resistance is mediated by mTORC2 loss and uncoupled from longevity" — referenced in the current paper's prose ("chronic rapamycin dosing beyond approximately 2 weeks disrupts mTORC2"); verify DOI before quote in references block.
- Lamming, *Cold Spring Harb Perspect Med* 2016 review on mTORC1 vs mTORC2 in aging — verify before quote.
- Mannick & Lamming, *Nature Aging* 2023 (above).
- Schreiber et al., *Aging Cell* 2015, "Rapamycin-mediated mTORC2 inhibition is determined by the relative expression of FK506-binding proteins" — verify before quote.

**What Lamming would criticize in our paper.**
1. The current Methods section cites the 2-week mTORC2 threshold but the Discussion does not name the causal mechanism (FKBP12 / FKBP51 / Rictor stoichiometry) the threshold derives from — a senior reviewer reads this as borrowed shorthand without actual engagement.
2. The Stanfield 2026 mixed-HbA1c finding maps directly onto Lamming's prediction (chronic mTORC2 disruption produces glycemic dysregulation) but the paper does not name Lamming as the framework that *predicted* this result. Failing to credit the predictive framework when our corpus instantiates it is the kind of citation behavior that flags a reviewer.
3. Treating "mixed HbA1c" as ambiguous noise rather than as evidence for the Lamming framework is interpretively passive.

**What our corpus supports / challenges.**
- *Supports Lamming:* Stanfield 2026's bidirectional HbA1c effects under weekly + exercise dosing are compatible with partial mTORC2 disruption emerging at cumulative-exposure thresholds that depend on co-intervention.
- *Challenges Lamming:* Kell 2026's five-week observational mTOR-signaling reduction did not report adverse glycemic markers, suggesting either (a) the two-week threshold is dose-cumulative not duration-cumulative, or (b) immune-cell mTORC2 disruption manifests differently than hepatic/muscle mTORC2 disruption.
- *Post-rescue check:* Lamming 2012 should be a directly cited mechanistic receipt, not just a 2-week-threshold token in prose; expect to surface 2–4 mechanistic receipts in the 34-receipt corpus that map to this framework.

**Proposed engagement section language.**

> Lamming's framework (Lamming 2012; Mannick & Lamming 2023) predicts that rapamycin's geroprotective benefit is mTORC1-mediated but its glycemic and reproductive toxicity is mTORC2-mediated and emerges with cumulative exposure beyond approximately two weeks. The bidirectional HbA1c signal in Stanfield 2026 is compatible with partial mTORC2 disruption under intermittent + exercise co-intervention; our corpus thus instantiates rather than challenges Lamming's prediction. The clinical implication, following Lamming, is that the metric of interest in human geroprotective trials is not "did HbA1c shift" but "did mTORC2 substrates (Akt-Ser473, PKC) remain intact," which is currently unmeasured in our receipt set.

## 3. Kennedy — "geroscience-first interventions are most cost-effective if validated against multiple outcome classes simultaneously"

**Core thesis.** Brian Kennedy (NUS Centre for Healthy Longevity; previously Buck Institute) argues for the geroscience hypothesis at the level of *trial design*: aging interventions should be evaluated against multi-outcome composite endpoints that span cardiometabolic, immune, cognitive, and physical-function domains, because the predicted benefit is correlated improvement across all of them. A single-domain trial, by his logic, is structurally unable to detect a true geroprotective signal because the benefit is split across measurements.

**Anchor papers.**
- Kennedy et al., *Cell* 2014, "Geroscience: linking aging to chronic disease" — verify before quote.
- Kennedy & Lamming, *Cell Metab* 2016, on mTOR in aging — verify before quote.
- Kennedy et al., *Aging Cell* 2020, on rapamycin trial design considerations — verify before quote.

**What Kennedy would criticize in our paper.**
1. The synthesis is single-outcome-class (cardiometabolic only in AAA4); the geroscience argument requires composite endpoints. Restricting to one outcome class structurally guarantees null-or-mixed results because the underlying signal is spread across orthogonal domains.
2. The "boundary conditions" framing is correct but underdeveloped: Kennedy would expect a paper that names the *minimum* cross-domain signal required to declare geroprotective benefit, not just a list of unresolved variables.
3. Lack of a power calculation against composite versus single-domain endpoints is a methodological gap a Kennedy-aligned reviewer would flag.

**What our corpus supports / challenges.**
- *Supports Kennedy:* The mechanistic-clinical translation gap our paper identifies is precisely Kennedy's argument that single-domain trials underdetect geroscience benefits; we are reaching his conclusion via a different route.
- *Challenges Kennedy:* Our corpus does not yet contain a multi-outcome rapamycin RCT; Kennedy's framework recommends one but our synthesis cannot evaluate one if it does not exist.
- *Post-rescue check:* Expansion to 34 receipts likely surfaces multi-outcome observational and animal studies; the paper should add a Kennedy-style "minimum composite signal" prediction as a falsifiable trial-design recommendation.

**Proposed engagement section language.**

> Kennedy's geroscience framework (Kennedy 2014; Kennedy & Lamming 2016) holds that the geroprotective signal of rapamycin is correlated across cardiometabolic, immune, cognitive, and physical-function domains, and that single-domain trials structurally underdetect benefit. Our corpus instantiates Kennedy's critique: the cardiometabolic-only outcome class in the curated receipts cannot, by construction, evaluate his composite-benefit prediction. We therefore frame the next-trial recommendation in Kennedy's terms — a composite endpoint trial pre-specifying a minimum cross-domain effect-size threshold, rather than a single-domain power calculation.

## 4. Kaeberlein — "the dog aging project is the cleanest rapamycin healthspan test outside controlled rodents"

**Core thesis.** Matt Kaeberlein (Optispan; previously UW Healthy Aging & Longevity Research Institute) argues that rapamycin's healthspan effects in companion animals (Dog Aging Project) are the most direct human-relevant evidence outside rodents: dogs share the human environment, develop spontaneous age-related disease, and are large enough to permit owner-reported functional outcomes. He has been publicly skeptical of human rapamycin trials that lack age-stratified enrolment and pre-specified frailty endpoints.

**Anchor papers.**
- Kaeberlein et al., *Geroscience* 2016, "Rapamycin in companion animals" — verify before quote.
- Urfer et al., *Geroscience* 2017, "A randomized controlled trial to establish effects of short-term rapamycin treatment in 24 middle-aged companion dogs" — verify before quote.
- Creevy et al., *Nature* 2022, "An open science study of ageing in companion dogs" (DAP cohort design) — verify before quote.

**What Kaeberlein would criticize in our paper.**
1. The synthesis treats Harrison 2009 (mouse ITP) as the only preclinical longevity anchor; companion-dog evidence is more translationally informative and is missing.
2. The lack of an age-stratification or baseline-frailty discussion mirrors the human-trial design gap Kaeberlein has been publicly critical about.
3. The paper does not engage the "right population" question (who benefits most from rapamycin) which is a Kaeberlein-flagged issue.

**What our corpus supports / challenges.**
- *Supports Kaeberlein:* Our discussion correctly identifies population specificity and baseline frailty as unresolved questions, which aligns with Kaeberlein's design critique.
- *Challenges Kaeberlein:* Our corpus contains zero companion-animal evidence; in his framework this is a structural omission, not a stylistic one.
- *Post-rescue check:* If the 34-receipt corpus admits Urfer 2017 or DAP-related papers, the paper can credibly discuss the dog cohort as a translational bridge between Harrison 2009 and human trials.

**Proposed engagement section language.**

> Kaeberlein's framework (Kaeberlein 2016; Urfer 2017; Creevy 2022) holds that the most translationally informative evidence outside rodents is companion-animal data, where dogs share the human environment and develop spontaneous age-related disease. Our corpus does not contain companion-animal evidence; we note this as a structural gap rather than a contested interpretation. The Dog Aging Project's pre-specified frailty and functional endpoints offer a template for the human trial design our synthesis recommends, particularly for age-stratified enrolment and owner-reported outcome measures.

## 5. Harrison / ITP — "the canonical preclinical signal is replicable across sites and dosing strategies"

**Core thesis.** David Harrison (Jackson Laboratory; NIA Interventions Testing Program) and the multi-site ITP collaboration (Strong, Miller, Nadon) argue that rapamycin's lifespan-extension finding is the most robust preclinical longevity signal in mammalian biology because it has been replicated at three independent ITP sites, across multiple dose regimens, in heterogeneous-stock mice, and starting at multiple ages. Their framework treats inter-site replicability as the binding evidence standard, not effect size.

**Anchor papers.**
- Harrison et al., *Nature* 2009 (DOI: 10.1038/nature08221 — in current bundle).
- Miller et al., *Aging Cell* 2014, "Rapamycin-mediated lifespan increase in mice is dose and sex dependent" — verify before quote.
- Strong et al., *Aging Cell* 2020, on encapsulated rapamycin in heterogeneous mice — verify before quote.
- Bitto et al., *eLife* 2016, "Transient rapamycin treatment can increase lifespan and healthspan in middle-aged mice" — verify before quote.

**What Harrison/ITP would criticize in our paper.**
1. The current paper cites Harrison 2009 as a single number ("approximately 14% in males, 9% in females") rather than as a *replicated multi-site signal*. The replication is the load-bearing claim.
2. The mid-life vs late-life dosing question (Bitto 2016 transient dosing) is unaddressed — this matters because human trials cannot ethically enroll lifelong-exposure cohorts and need preclinical guidance on intermittent windows.
3. The dose-response curve from Miller 2014 (3 mg/kg → 14 mg/kg) is the strongest preclinical dose-finding evidence and is missing.

**What our corpus supports / challenges.**
- *Supports Harrison/ITP:* The mechanistic-to-clinical translation gap our paper identifies is consistent with the ITP framework: the preclinical signal is real, the clinical translation requires different dosing.
- *Challenges Harrison/ITP:* Our corpus has zero ITP follow-on papers; it cannot evaluate the replication-as-evidence-standard claim.
- *Post-rescue check:* Expect Miller 2014 and Strong 2020 to enter as mechanistic receipts in the 34-receipt corpus; if they do not, retrieval calibration for the ITP cohort is broken.

**Proposed engagement section language.**

> The ITP framework (Harrison 2009; Miller 2014; Strong 2020) treats inter-site replicability as the binding standard for preclinical longevity evidence: rapamycin extends lifespan in heterogeneous-stock mice at three independent sites, under multiple dose regimens, starting at multiple ages, including mid-life onset (Bitto 2016). Our paper cites Harrison 2009 as the canonical single number; the ITP framework would have us cite the replication itself as the primary signal. The unanswered translational question, on this framework, is which mid-life or late-life intermittent dose schedule in mice maps onto the weekly low-dose human regimen — a question our corpus does not yet address.

## 6. Selman — "S6K1 deletion is the genetic complement to rapamycin"

**Core thesis.** Colin Selman (University of Glasgow) and collaborators argue that the genetic phenocopy of rapamycin (S6K1 knockout) extends female mouse lifespan and mimics dietary-restriction effects, providing genetic evidence that mTORC1-S6K1 axis modulation is sufficient for longevity benefit, independent of pharmacology. The framework constrains rapamycin's mechanism: lifespan benefit must operate through the mTORC1-S6K1 branch, not through off-target effects.

**Anchor papers.**
- Selman et al., *Science* 2009, "Ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1 signaling regulates mammalian life span" — verify before quote.
- Selman & Withers, *Antioxid Redox Signal* 2011, "Mammalian models of extended lifespan" — verify before quote.

**What Selman would criticize in our paper.**
1. No genetic evidence is cited for the mechanism. A reviewer aligned with Selman would want at least one S6K1-knockout reference to ground the claim that mTORC1 inhibition is sufficient for longevity.
2. The female-specific S6K1 effect (Selman 2009) is a sex-stratification anchor that maps onto the Harrison 2009 9% female / 14% male asymmetry — the paper currently treats sex as an unresolved variable rather than naming the mechanistic link.

**What our corpus supports / challenges.**
- *Supports Selman:* Our paper identifies sex-stratified human data as a gap; this is exactly the prediction Selman's framework makes.
- *Challenges Selman:* Our corpus does not contain genetic evidence; we cannot evaluate the genetic-pharmacological convergence claim directly.
- *Post-rescue check:* If Selman 2009 enters via the mechanism-paper retrieval lane, the genetic complement is a one-paragraph addition to Background.

**Proposed engagement section language.**

> The Selman genetic framework (Selman 2009) provides the orthogonal evidence that mTORC1-S6K1 axis modulation is sufficient for mammalian lifespan extension independent of pharmacology, with female-specific lifespan effects that parallel the Harrison 2009 ITP sex asymmetry. Our corpus does not directly address the genetic complement; we note this as a one-step extension in Background and as the most parsimonious explanation for the unresolved sex-stratification gap our Limitations section identifies.

## 7. Anisimov — "independent replication outside the ITP, including in non-heterogeneous strains"

**Core thesis.** Vladimir Anisimov (Petrov Institute; deceased 2019) and collaborators independently replicated rapamycin's lifespan-extension signal in non-heterogeneous mouse strains (including 129/Sv) using non-encapsulated formulations and different dosing routes, providing independent confirmation outside the NIA ITP framework. The framework's relevance is *external validity*: rapamycin's effect is not specific to the heterogeneous-stock JAX colony or the encapsulated-feed dosing strategy.

**Anchor papers.**
- Anisimov et al., *Cell Cycle* 2011, "Rapamycin extends maximal lifespan in cancer-prone mice" — verify before quote.
- Anisimov et al., *Aging* 2010 / 2011, on rapamycin in 129/Sv mice — verify before quote.
- Komarova et al., *Aging* 2012, "Rapamycin extends lifespan and delays tumorigenesis in heterozygous p53+/- mice" — verify before quote.

**What Anisimov would criticize in our paper.**
1. The preclinical case rests entirely on Harrison 2009; without Anisimov-cohort replication, the paper appears to be relying on a single laboratory consortium.
2. The cancer-prone-mouse replication (Komarova 2012) is directly relevant to whether rapamycin's lifespan benefit operates through cancer prevention versus aging deceleration — a question our paper does not engage.

**What our corpus supports / challenges.**
- *Supports Anisimov:* The mechanistic-to-clinical translation gap is consistent with multiple-strain preclinical replication being a precondition for human trials, which Anisimov-style replication satisfies.
- *Challenges Anisimov:* Our corpus contains no Anisimov-cohort papers; the external-validity argument cannot be made.
- *Post-rescue check:* If the 34-receipt corpus surfaces independent-replication papers, the paper can move from "Harrison 2009 says X" to "the canonical signal replicates outside ITP," which is a stronger claim.

**Proposed engagement section language.**

> Independent replication outside the NIA ITP — Anisimov 2011 in 129/Sv mice, Komarova 2012 in cancer-prone mice — addresses the external-validity question of whether rapamycin's lifespan-extension effect is specific to the heterogeneous-stock JAX colony. Our corpus does not yet contain these replications; we identify this as a corpus expansion target. The conceptual implication, on the Anisimov framework, is that the preclinical signal generalises across strains and dosing routes, raising the prior probability of clinical translation.

## 8. López-Otín — "the hallmarks of aging integrate rapamycin into a multi-pathway target landscape"

**Core thesis.** Carlos López-Otín (Universidad de Oviedo) and collaborators frame aging as a set of interconnected hallmarks (originally nine in 2013, expanded to twelve in 2023). In this framework, rapamycin's specific intervention point is "deregulated nutrient sensing," and its effects propagate through cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered intercellular communication. The framework's value is integrative: it locates rapamycin in a hallmark hierarchy rather than treating it as a standalone pharmacological intervention.

**Anchor papers.**
- López-Otín et al., *Cell* 2013, "The hallmarks of aging" (DOI: 10.1016/j.cell.2013.05.039) — verify before quote.
- López-Otín et al., *Cell* 2023, "Hallmarks of aging: an expanding universe" — verify before quote.

**What López-Otín would criticize in our paper.**
1. The hallmarks framework is the most-cited integrative framework in geroscience and is not engaged; the paper's "geroscience hypothesis" framing is correct but un-grounded in the canonical citation.
2. The integration of rapamycin's mTORC1 effects with downstream hallmarks (senescence, mitochondrial function, autophagy) is missing — Kell 2026's DNA-damage finding maps directly onto a hallmark (genomic instability) but is not framed that way.

**What our corpus supports / challenges.**
- *Supports López-Otín:* Kell 2026's mechanistic finding fits cleanly into the genomic-instability hallmark; the paper's mechanistic-clinical gap is consistent with a hallmark-framework prediction that downstream phenotype takes longer to emerge than upstream pathway modulation.
- *Challenges López-Otín:* Our corpus does not have multi-hallmark data on rapamycin; we cannot evaluate the integrative claim.
- *Post-rescue check:* López-Otín 2023 should enter as a background framework citation; receipt-level hallmark mapping is a corpus-expansion target.

**Proposed engagement section language.**

> The López-Otín hallmarks framework (López-Otín 2013; López-Otín 2023) locates rapamycin's intervention point at "deregulated nutrient sensing" and predicts downstream propagation through genomic instability, cellular senescence, and altered intercellular communication. Kell 2026's finding of enhanced resilience against DNA damage maps onto the genomic-instability hallmark; this is the strongest hallmark-level signal in our corpus. The framework's prediction — that mTORC1 inhibition produces correlated improvement across multiple hallmarks rather than isolated pathway modulation — is consistent with our mechanistic-to-clinical translation gap and with Kennedy's composite-endpoint argument. We adopt the López-Otín hallmarks as the integrative scaffold for the Discussion's mechanism-to-clinic narrative.

## Cross-framework synthesis: where the frameworks agree and disagree

| Framework | Position on intermittent dosing | Position on cardiometabolic-only outcomes | Position on translational sufficiency of mouse data |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mannick | Necessary for mTORC1 selectivity | Wrong primary endpoint | Insufficient — need immune-aging human data |
| Lamming | Necessary to spare mTORC2 | Predicts mixed signal under intermittent + co-intervention | Necessary but not sufficient — need pharmacodynamic human data |
| Kennedy | Compatible with composite endpoints | Structurally underdetects benefit | Insufficient — need composite human endpoints |
| Kaeberlein | Compatible with companion-animal validation | Insufficient without functional/frailty endpoints | Insufficient — need companion-animal data |
| Harrison/ITP | Replicated across sites and ages | Out of scope (preclinical) | Sufficient as preclinical evidence; clinical translation requires different dosing |
| Selman | Genetic complement does not require dosing | N/A | Sufficient at the mechanistic level; sex asymmetry predicted |
| Anisimov | Replicated outside ITP cohort | Out of scope (preclinical) | External-validity confirmed; clinical translation open |
| López-Otín | Compatible | Insufficient — need multi-hallmark data | Mouse data is one hallmark-level test among many |

**Convergence across frameworks (load-bearing claims for the paper):**
1. Intermittent low-dose dosing is mechanistically necessary (Mannick + Lamming).
2. Cardiometabolic-only outcomes structurally underdetect geroprotective benefit (Mannick + Kennedy + Kaeberlein + López-Otín).
3. Sex stratification is a predicted, not noise-level, variable (Harrison + Selman).
4. Preclinical-to-clinical translation requires different dosing strategies, not different drugs (all eight frameworks, with varying emphasis).

**Divergence (load-bearing tensions for the paper):**
1. Mannick (immune-aging-first) vs Kennedy (composite-first) on which endpoint to prioritize.
2. Kaeberlein (companion-animal first) vs Mannick/Kennedy (human-trial first) on the next-step evidence.
3. ITP (replication-as-standard) vs López-Otín (integration-as-standard) on what counts as sufficient evidence.

These convergences and divergences should structure the paper's "Engagement with Established Frameworks" section as a *navigation* of the field, not a list of names.
